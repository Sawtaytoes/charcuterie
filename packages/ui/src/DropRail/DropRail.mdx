# DropRail

Every destination at once, brought to the pointer instead of the pointer being carried to

## The measurement this exists for

`Board` moves a card with a `Menu` on the card, and for a board that is the right control:
three or four lanes, all of them on screen, and the menu opens where your hand already is.

It stops being the right control at the size the fleet's real lists reach. Docket's Backlog
draws **34 project groups down one page**, of which about four fit on a display. Dragging a
card from the first group to the thirty-fourth means holding a pointer down while the page
scrolls under it, and a menu of 34 items is a scrolling list inside a popup — which is the
same problem in a smaller box.

A rail answers both halves. It pins to the top for the length of the move, so no destination
is ever off screen, and there is no travel: every destination is the same short distance
away.

## One control, three drivers

WCAG 2.5.7 (AA since 2.2) requires a single-pointer alternative to any dragging movement,
and `FileDropZone` says the same thing about its own gesture: build the accessible path
first and let the drag be the enhancement.

So this is **not** a drag affordance with a fallback bolted on. It is a listbox that three
things drive, all committing through one `onPick`:

| Driver | Active chip | Commits on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pointer | the chip under the pointer, while a button is held | release over a chip |
| Tap | the chip you touch | the tap |
| Keyboard | walked with the arrow keys | <kbd>Enter</kbd> |

The rail does not know which of the three opened it, and the host does not have to say. It
reads `event.buttons` on the first pointer event it sees — a button already down means a
drag is in flight.

## It takes no drag-and-drop dependency

The [board's decision](https://github.com/Sawtaytoes/charcuterie) priced four drag-and-drop
libraries and took none of them, because a published library's dependency list is inherited
by every consumer — including one with 60 KB gzipped to spend in total. Nothing here changes
that arithmetic, and this component needs far less than `Board` does: a chip is a box under
the pointer, so `elementFromPoint` answers the only geometric question there is. No
snapshot, no collision strategy, no sortable transform.

## It is not `Rail`

`Rail` is the app shell's side rail — a landmark, an `<aside>` or a `<nav>`, permanent
furniture down one edge of a page. `DropRail` is a horizontal strip of drop targets that
exists only for the length of a move, and it is a `listbox`. They share four letters and
nothing else; `FileDropZone` is the precedent for the prefix carrying the distinction.

## The three chip states

<Canvas of={DropRailStories.AllStates} />

The destination the item is **already in** stays visible and unofferable rather than being
filtered out. A rail whose contents change depending on what you grabbed is a rail whose
chips move under the pointer between one move and the next, and muscle memory is the only
reason this control is faster than a menu.

Neither that state nor the disabled one is marked by colour alone. The current chip carries
visually hidden text — *"— where it is now"* — and both carry `aria-disabled`, because the
one chip a person must not aim for is exactly the one that must not rely on being dimmer
(WCAG 1.4.1).

## What it deliberately does not do

It does not render the thing being dragged, own the handle, or know what a move means. The
host keeps all three, which is what lets the same rail sit above a board, a card grid, or a
list of accordions without any of them being modelled here.
